Energy imports and renewable energy: A multi-country analysis

By J.T. McDermott

Goethe Universität MIEEP

10 July 2026

1. Introduction

Energy is self-evidently one of the most important commodities in a modern economy. On a national level, governments, businesses, and individuals face a number of decisions and tradeoffs when considering energy production, trade, and policy. Among these considerations are the import intensity of energy supplies and the domestic employment of renewable, green technology sources.

National energy trade balances have become particularly salient in recent years due to a number of high-profile energy shocks, which have affected both countries reliant on imported energy in the form of crude oil and natural gas, as well as exporters who depend on revenue, namely the 2022 energy crisis in the wake of the Russian invasion of Ukraine and the 2026 energy shock caused by the conflict between the United States and Israel against Iran. The 2022 shock saw global prices for crude oil increase by 40%. Europe, particularly dependent on natural gas imports from Russia, faced an increase of 180% in natural gas prices, significantly impacting economic activity and requiring emergency policies (Adolfsen, et al. 2022). The closure of the Strait of Hormuz in March of 2026, through which some 20% of global crude oil and natural gas supplies flow similarly resulted in sharp increases in energy prices and even physical shortages requiring interventions from governments (IEA. "Strait of Hormuz - About", Carbon Brief. “Iran War Analysis …”). These recent examples, as well as other historical examples such as the 1941 US embargo of oil against Japan, 1973 oil embargo by OPEC, the 1979 Iranian Revolution, 1990 Gulf War, and ongoing US blockade of oil to Cuba demonstrate how energy can be weaponized in international conflicts and how vulnerable supplies are in times of geopolitical tension. In a world where geopolitical uncertainty is ostensibly increasing, policymakers, business leaders, and individuals must carefully assess their dependence on energy imports in order to protect national economic and security interests.

Another related, salient issue in policy and economic circles is climate change, and the global shortfall in green investment required to meet climate goals (Kahn 2025). Energy production comprises a significant amount of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions which cause climate change (IEA. "Greenhouse Gas Emissions from Energy Data Explorer - Data Tools"), so in recent decades there has been an observed increase in discussion and investment in green, renewable energy production by companies and governments into technologies such as solar panels, wind turbines, hydroelectric dams, and battery storage.

Given these two imperative global trends relating to national energy mix, one may theorize that there is a connection between a country’s import intensity of energy and their renewable investment decisions. It is possible to imagine two scenarios that illustrate a potential link. First, countries that have high import dependence may seek to increase domestically produced renewable energy production and substitute away from fossil fuel imports to reduce risk from energy shocks. Second, countries that are serious about mitigating the impact of climate change via renewable energy investments may also experience a reduction of fossil imports by virtue of increasing domestic renewable production. This paper aims to understand the relationship in the first situation using a panel data regression of 139 countries over a period 1990 to 2022. The goal is to see if a country’s net energy imports tell us anything about the level of renewable investment in said country.

2. Overview of the Global Energy Trade and Renewable Energy Investment

The World’s largest current primary energy source is fossil fuels, accounting for 80% of total energy supply (TES) (IEA. "World"). By geological chance, these fossil fuel endowments are not distributed evenly across different countries. Instead, they are concentrated in a few specific regions and countries. The number of countries with fossil fuel reserves sufficient enough to be significant exporters to the global markets is even lower. In Figure 1, the geographic concentration can be seen plainly: countries like Saudi Arabia, the United States, Russia, and Canada dominate. In fact, the top five oil exporters (Saudi Arabia, United States, Canada, and United Arab Emirates) account for roughly 50% of all global exports, and the top ten (top five and Iraq, Norway, Nigeria, Brazil, Kuwait) bring that number to roughly 70%. Overall, there are more countries that are net importers of energy supplies than net exporters.

The high concentration of suppliers of energy constitutes a risk for importing countries, particularly to short-term disruptions. The concentration of risk is even higher considering regional interconnections. For instance, up to the year until 2022, European countries developed a significant dependence on natural gas imports from Russia (Consilium. "Where does the EU's gas come from?”), and these imports traveled mainly via pipeline. The Russian invasion of Ukraine and resulting disruption to natural gas flows to Europe caused a large, short term spike in prices in Europe. Long term, substituting away from these cheaper pipeline imports toward seaborne LNG imports from the US and Middle East has required significant investment in infrastructure such as specialized terminals, and overall increased unit prices for imports, resulting in a structural increase in energy prices in Europe. This structural increase in prices has hampered the international competitiveness of the German economy in particular.

In general, as can be seen in Figure 2, Europe, South Asia, and East Asia are the regions most visibly dependent on imported energy imports. In ‘normal’ times this arrangement makes sense: Europe and East Asia specialize in high tech manufacturing and trade those goods for energy from countries with large natural fossil fuel endowments. However, when oil and gas prices experience volatility or even physical shortages, the effects can be rapid and severe.

Looking at renewable energy share by country, most countries are still relatively low. In my dataset, 62 out of 139 countries considered were below 20% renewable share, and 107 out of 139 were below 50% renewable share as of 2021. Note that I am considering final renewable energy consumption as a percentage of the total versus percent of electricity generation done with renewable energy, a related but more specific measure that does not consider the significant energy use from transportation with fossil fuels. A band of countries with a high renewable share can also be observed in Africa. However, this can be explained primarily by the use of biofuels like plant and waste material, not as much the use of modern techniques like solar, wind, and hydroelectric. When considering only what the International Energy Agency (IEA) considers ‘modern’ renewable technology, there are some stand out countries like Gabon with 66%, Eswatini with 39%, and Uganda with 35%, primarily driven by hydroelectric power (IEA. "Africa - Countries & Regions").

While some countries have made great strides toward increasing renewable energy share, many large, rich, industrialized countries still lag behind, which matters because these countries are historically and currently the largest contributors to GHG emissions. If we consider the top three global economies, the United States, China, and the European Union, their renewable shares are only 9% (USEIA. "U.S. energy facts explained - consumption and production"), 25% (IEA. "China - Countries & Regions"), and 25% (EEA. "Share of energy consumption from renewable sources in Europe"). The global share of total final energy consumption produced with renewable sources was between 17.9% and 20% as of 2022 depending on the source, with roughly 28% of renewable supply being ‘traditional use of biomass’, or 4.9% of total energy production (UN Statistics "SDG Indicators").

Figures 4-7 below show a side-by-side comparison of import intensity and renewable share in China, the US, and the EU over time. In China, import intensity has risen as demand for energy rapidly eclipsed domestic supplies. There is a U-shaped trend of renewable use, reflecting the decreased proportion of biomass fuel use, increased demand for coal and oil to support the rapid industrialisation of the 1990s through the 2000s, while the recent uptick reflects China’s push toward electrification and specific success in solar power and electric auto buildout.

The EU has seen a steady import intensity, with a slight increase in the 2010s. Renewable use has increased notably thanks to a broad push in the block to decarbonize.

For decades, the US was a net energy importer, relying on imports of crude oil in particular from Canada, Venezuela, and the Middle East. However, in the early 2020s, the US became a net exporter again, in large part due to the so-called ‘Shale Revolution’, a technological development  that unlocked previously unexploitable oil and gas reserves. Renewable share is comparatively low but has been increasing steadily over time.

3. Literature Review

In this section, I will provide an overview of some of the existing literature with respect to determinants of green investment, the effect of energy crises on green energy investment, and the relationship between the energy imports and renewable energy use at a national level.

3.1. Determinants of Green Finance

The increasing importance of climate change in public discourse has corresponded to an increase in interest in the field of green finance. There is a wide consensus that there is a significant gap in global annual investment in green transition tech necessary to achieve the goals of the green transition (Kahn 2025). Even countries ostensibly doing well at marshalling green finance such as Germany are still facing a gap on the order of billions of dollars/euros per year (Brand, et al. 2021).

A number of structural factors that determine green investment levels have been identified in a large breadth of existing literature. Naturally, financial considerations such as investor preferences and perceptions, risk of projects, and interest rate levels are key determinants to levels of green investment (Cilekoglu and Yilmaz 2026, Engel, et al. 2024).

Structural and policy level factors are also critical determinants. “The robust predictors of green finance are globalization, GDP per capita, trade, multilateral flows, climate vulnerability, government effectiveness, rule of law, official development assistance, unemployment, and population,” according to Grechyna and Ofori 2025, lending credence to the hypothesis that energy trade dynamics may affect renewable energy deployment, as well as indicating potential controls for this paper, especially GDP per capita. The work of Li, et al. 2026 shows that domestic economic policy uncertainty in a country is associated with lower green investments in G7 countries from 1995 to 2022, and the work of Liew, et al. 2025 shows that government funding combined with policies like carbon taxes and carbon laws can encourage institutional investors to increase green investments.

3.2. Energy Security and Green Energy Investment

The existing academic literature about energy prices and crises effect on green and renewable energy investment broadly points to the conclusion that renewable investments respond to international energy markets, particularly oil markets, albeit with some caveats in defining the mechanisms and accounting for differences in geography and different levels of development.

Depending on a country’s income level, the response differs per Ben Cheikh and Ben Zaied 2024. They find that, of the 38 countries surveyed, lower income countries tend to see a negative effect of geopolitical risk, measured with an index taken from Caldara et al. 2023 (reissued in 2026), on the use of renewable energy, while wealthier countries see an opposite effect. As far as differences between developed countries, Shah, et al. 2018 compared Norway, the UK, and the US, and found different impulse responses of renewable investment to oil prices. Kim, et al. 2025 demonstrated that countries that do increase renewable energy share do experience an increase in energy security. Dutta, et al. 2020 explores the relationship between oil price movements and green asset prices, and finds that there is a positive but statistically insignificant relationship between oil price and green asset prices. However, they do find a significant positive relationship between oil price volatility and green asset prices, indicating that investors are more responsive to uncertainty in energy markets than higher but stable prices. In an analysis limited to the developing and emerging economies of South Asia, Murshed and Tanha 2020 provide further evidence that lower income countries may have difficulty implementing renewable transition in response to oil price, but that sufficiently high oil prices along with economic growth encourages higher renewable adoption in developing economies. In China specifically, for the period 1990-2020, oil price increases are associated with an increase in renewable energy consumption (Mukhtarov 2024), a key finding considering China’s economic size and importance in the green transition.

One might suspect that countries trying to reduce import dependence will also increase domestic fossil fuel which may offset any environmental gains from increased renewable use. There is a large literature that explores the role of this so-called “rebound effect,” such as Chang, et al. 2025, which indicates that countries must reach a certain threshold of renewable consumption before structural substitution with fossil fuels begins to occur. In an analysis of 26 EU countries from 2007-2016, Carfora, et al. 2022 showed that renewable investments are more effective than fossil fuel investments at increasing energy security.  

3.3. Energy Imports and Green Investment

In contrast to this paper which seeks to understand the potential causal link between import reliance and subsequent investment in green energy technology, Vaona 2015 Estimates the reverse and finds that increased renewable energy production actually reduces import demand growth. In emerging economies like China, India, and Brazil over the period 1996 to 2019, Yadav and Mahalik 2024 shows a clear negative relationship between renewable energy development and import dependence. A similar analysis of European countries in Kukharets, et al. 2023 corroborates this finding. Rasoulinezad 2025 takes a more indirect approach by considering the effect of green tax policies and how subsequent increases in renewable energy use reduces import dependence in emerging countries.

The most important reference for this analysis by far is Makki, et al. 2024. Their research design corresponds closely with the inquiry of this paper. They investigate the factors that drive the use of renewable energy sources at the country level, including economic growth, oil-rents, and a country’s net energy imports. They split their data into segments, net exporters and net exporters, in order to compare their outcomes, and they used multiple regression analysis fixed effects. Their most relevant finding for our analysis here is that increased energy imports decrease renewable energy consumption in both oil importing and oil exporting countries

4. Data and Methodology

4.1 Data

All of the data in this analysis is sourced from the World Bank Open Data database. My analysis includes data of net energy imports as a percent of total consumption, GDP per capita in constant US dollars, GDP growth, and CO2 emissions per capita. The panel is constructed with data from 139 countries over a period from 1990 to 2022.

4.2 Methodology

My methodology takes significant inspiration from that of Makki, et al. 2024, with some important modifications in order to provide a novel analysis. In brief, Makki, et al. 2024 only considers 24 countries and then divides the countries into two different groups: net importers and net exporters. I was able to construct a data set with different controls and thus more countries, and I decided to keep all of the countries together because I am not specifically interested in the differences between net importers and exporters, and some countries change between importers and exporters through the time series, such as the United States. I take log(GDP per capita) in order to make a regression with renewable share tractable. The general specification can be observed below:

 I opted for a country and time fixed effects in order to understand how, within a country, the net energy imports affect the share of renewable energy used. Using country fixed effects enables controlling for structural differences between countries like geography, and time fixed effects allow for controlling of global shocks, such as the 2015 Paris Climate accord, which likely inspired a global change in renewable energy adoption. I also use country-clustered standard errors to account for heteroskedasticity between country errors.

As my original goal was to understand green energy investment, I also decided to analyze lagged versions of my model. Since I am unable to construct an adequate green energy investment panel for multiple countries, this can serve as an imperfect proxy. The idea is that, since renewable projects may take multiple years to bring online, changes in renewable use in a country may be delayed relative to their import intensity. I analyze lags on net energy imports of one, three, and five years.

5. Empirical Findings

In the table below, I provide the results of the five separate regressions: with country fixed effects only, country and year fixed effects, and the three lagged models with lags of one, three, and five years respectively.

5.1 Findings with Respect to Net Energy Imports

I find that there is no statistically significant relation between net energy imports and renewable share in the same-period analysis or lagged analysis of one and three years. However, there is a statistically significant, albeit small effect in the lagged model for five years, suggesting that a one percent increase in net imports corresponds to a 0.004 percent increase in renewable share in five years. The significance of this finding is diminished by limits in the design of this model and availability of data, but it does suggest at least some amount of variation in renewable energy can be plausibly traced back to energy import dynamics. Yet the effect is small, and the lag of five periods significantly reduced the number of observations, further decreasing the validity of the result.

5.2 Findings with Respect to Other Variables

I find a significant negative relation between log(GDP per capita) and renewable energy share. This likely is a result of a) high usage of biomass fuels in poor and developing countries pushing up their renewable shares and b) the still relatively low renewable share of highly developed regions like Europe and North America. GDP growth also has a significant effect on renewable share, which follows from the fact that a growing economy is necessary to make the investments necessary to convert to renewable energy. CO2 emissions per capita is also, as expected, negatively associated with renewable energy share, given that renewable energy sources are designed to reduce carbon emissions.

6. Conclusions

By linking the topical and important issues of renewable energy transition and energy import dependence, my hope is to increase understanding of both simultaneously and provide insights for decision makers in policy and the energy economy. Unfortunately, the empirical findings of this paper are light on policy recommendations, other than perhaps recommending an amount of patience in implementation and expectations of green energy investments. Two things remain clear that have been indicated numerous times already: the link between GDP and renewable share needs to be reversed, and likely will be reversed as more and more renewable projects come online in the coming decades. National governments should also find ways to reduce energy import reliance while also avoiding“all of the above”, that is, increasing all forms of domestic energy production, a strategy that China and the US have implemented in recent years.

For further research, a more thorough analysis could be conducted with a better dataset, namely one constructed with actual renewable energy investment figures by country, one that could likely be constructed with the BloombergNEF database (though I wouldn’t know since I don’t have access to it). Further reading and analysis of individual country political economies would likely prove more insightful than trying to piece together the story in the aggregate data as I have done here. Tying the issue to political science would also be worthwhile, for instance, trying to understand if the salience of national defense concerns can encourage voters to support more investment in domestic renewable energy.
